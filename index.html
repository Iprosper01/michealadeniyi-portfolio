Journey

  1. 2017 – 2022 · Olabisi Onabanjo University

    Microbiology, first

    I trained as a microbiologist, learning disease and health systems from the inside before I ever touched a dataset. That order mattered later.

  2. 2022 · Helix Biogen Institute, Genomics Unit

    Where data entered the picture

    My internship placed me inside a genomics unit, and inside computational biology, for the first time — pathogen datasets, biostatistics, modelling. It was the first proof that biomedical questions could be answered with data as much as with a microscope.

  3. 3MTT

    Building the other half, deliberately

    I moved through 3MTT's track from data analysis into data science and machine learning — not to leave biomedical research behind, but to bring a second set of tools to it.

  4. HealingPro Technologies

    Where the two halves met

    I leveraged that biomedical domain knowledge to conceptualize SlideLabAI, an idea that became CellVue — an AI-powered malaria diagnostic and surveillance tool, and the flagship product of the startup I founded. I led the team that took it to a national hackathon win.

  5. CellVue

    What production actually required

    CellVue stalled before it reached production — outpaced for the sector's one viable customer by an established incumbent, without the risk capital to compete. It taught me that solving problems like this takes more than a good model.

  6. Now

    Two more prototypes, same conviction

    Today, under HealingPro Technologies, I'm building two more: SugarTalk and a USSD-based triage system. Both started the same way CellVue did — with a specific person, a specific failure, and a conviction that better data infrastructure could have changed the outcome.

Combines predictive machine learning with conversational AI — a Random Forest classifier tuned for screening sensitivity on non-invasive biometrics, paired with an LLM under clinical guardrails for personalized guidance and referrals.

  • Random Forest
  • LLM

Why I built it

My mother has managed diabetes for years, from a town without a tertiary hospital or a doctor equipped to treat it locally. Every month, sometimes twice, she travels to the Federal Medical Centre in the state capital for a routine that rarely changes: a blood pressure check, a blood sugar test, her weight and height for a BMI reading, then a short consultation before the trip home. It is a routine technology could handle for a fraction of the cost, the travel time, and the emissions of a trip that size, made this often, by people with no other option. SugarTalk is my attempt to bring that routine home.

  • USSD
  • SMS

Why it exists

During my time as a National Health Fellow in my LGA, a young woman in her twenties died of convulsions because help could not reach her in time. She had been managing an illness for days, and the distance and cost of the nearest health centre meant no one around her had taken her in. When she began convulsing, there was no way to reach the health centre quickly, or to reach anyone there for help. She lost her life to a gap in reach, not a gap in medicine. This system exists because closing that gap should not require a phone with internet, a car, or money up front — only a phone that can send a text.

Co-authored with Shamsuddeen Yusuf Ma'aruf, Joan Lusanji Imbwaga, Sulaimon Olawale Araromi, and Raphael Oyeniji · JMIR Preprints, under peer review

Bioinformatics Designing and Molecular Modelling of a Universal mRNA Vaccine for SARS-CoV-2 Infection

10.3390/vaccines10122107

Oladipo et al. · Vaccines (MDPI), 2022

Designed a candidate mRNA vaccine intended to protect against circulating SARS-CoV-2 variants and lineages across six continents, by screening viral sequences for optimal T-cell and B-cell epitopes. The resulting construct showed strong stability and activated the cell-mediated immune responses needed for durable protection, with an estimated population coverage above 68%.

The State of Digital Health Readiness in the Africa Health Workforce

10.5281/zenodo.22009775

InnovateHealth Africa, 2026

The first comprehensive, evidence-based assessment of digital health readiness among health workers across Sub-Saharan Africa, drawing on system-level indicators and workforce-level evidence from 48 countries between 2015 and 2026. Examines four structural pillars of readiness and sets out targeted recommendations for governments, training institutions, and implementing partners, establishing the baseline for a 2028 follow-up survey.
